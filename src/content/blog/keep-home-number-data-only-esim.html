You can keep your phone number with a travel eSIM because the eSIM only adds a data line next to your normal SIM. Your home SIM stays in the phone for calls and text messages, including bank codes, while the travel eSIM carries the internet. The trick is choosing which line does what, so you do not pay roaming fees by accident.

Key takeaways

  • A data-only eSIM has no phone number, so it never replaces the number your bank knows.
  • Dual SIM works on iPhone XS, XS Max, XR or later, and two carriers need an unlocked phone.
  • Set the travel eSIM for mobile data and keep the home SIM for calls and SMS.
  • Switch data roaming off on the home line, then check usage in your phone settings.
  • Test a bank code before you leave, not at the airport in another country.

What a data-only eSIM means for your number

Most travel eSIMs sell internet and nothing else. There is no local phone number attached, so nobody can call it and no text message can reach it. TonMobile, for example, is listed as data-only with no phone number, which is how most travel data plans are built.

That is good news for anyone worried about their number. Your own number lives on your home SIM, and a data-only eSIM does not touch it. You are adding a second line, not porting or swapping anything.

Home SIM

  • Holds your real phone number
  • Receives calls and SMS
  • Usually bills roaming outside your region

Data-only travel eSIM

  • No phone number
  • Carries mobile data only
  • Priced as a travel plan, not as roaming

How dual SIM works with a travel eSIM

Dual SIM means the phone holds two lines at once. One is usually your physical SIM, the other is an eSIM you install by QR code or in an app. Both stay registered, so the phone can use one for data and the other for calls and texts.

On iPhone, Apple states that Dual SIM, including eSIM, needs an iPhone XS, XS Max, XR or later. Apple also says that to use two carriers, the carrier must unlock the iPhone, unless both plans come from the same carrier (Apple Support, checked October 2026).

Carrier lock matters

If your phone is locked to your home carrier, a travel eSIM from another provider may refuse to install. Ask your carrier whether the phone is unlocked before you buy a plan. Our eSIM compatibility checklist walks through the other checks.

Android phones vary by brand and model, so look for the dual SIM or eSIM option in your phone's SIM settings, or ask the manufacturer. The same lock rule applies: check that the phone is not carrier-locked before you pay for a plan.

The settings to change on your phone

Once the eSIM is installed, label the two lines so you can tell them apart. Names such as "Home" and "Travel" save you from guessing later, when two lines look nearly identical in the menu.

  1. Name both lines

    Open the SIM or mobile service screen and rename the lines. Use something obvious like Home and Travel.

  2. Choose the line for mobile data

    Set the travel eSIM as the line for cellular data. Keep the home SIM as your default for calls and messages.

  3. Turn data roaming off on the home line

    Do this on the home SIM only. The travel eSIM needs its own data connection to work.

  4. Turn off automatic switching of data between lines

    If your phone offers an option that lets the other line carry data when signal is weak, disable it. Otherwise the home SIM may quietly use data and bill you.

To see which line is using data, open SettingsMobile Service on iPhone. On Android, open SettingsNetworkData usage. Check both lines after your first day.

Which line for data, which for calls

The simple rule is travel eSIM for data, home SIM for voice and SMS. Almost everything that costs real money abroad is mobile data on the home plan, so moving data to the eSIM removes most of the risk.

Avoid roaming charges on the home SIM

Switching data roaming off is the main protection, but it helps to know what your home plan covers. Inside the EU, "Roam Like At Home" applies in the 27 EU states plus Iceland, Liechtenstein and Norway, and from 1 January 2026 it also covers Ukraine and Moldova (European Commission, checked October 2026). Operators may still apply fair-use limits on data.

The UK is not covered

The EU roaming rules do not apply in the United Kingdom. The page we checked does not mention Switzerland, so confirm it with your operator. Never assume a border is free just because the previous country was.

Even when roaming is covered, your own operator decides the details. Ask them three things before you travel: which countries are included, whether a fair-use limit applies, and what happens to calls and texts when you step outside the covered area.

Bank SMS codes and two-factor logins

This is where people get nervous. Your bank sends a code to the number it has on file, which is your home number. A travel eSIM does not change that number, so the code still goes to your home SIM.

The question is whether the home SIM can receive it. That depends on signal, on your operator, and on whether your plan allows incoming texts abroad. Your operator can tell you, and it is worth asking before you leave.

Steps to make codes arrive

  1. Keep the home SIM switched on

    Do not remove it and do not turn the line off. Airplane mode with only Wi-Fi turned on also blocks the network on that line.

  2. Check whether incoming SMS works abroad

    Ask your operator about receiving text messages in your destination, and whether Wi-Fi calling or SMS over Wi-Fi is available on your plan.

  3. Test before you travel

    Log in to your banking app and request a code. Confirm it arrives while your phone is set up the way it will be abroad.

  4. Look for another login method

    Many banks offer an authenticator app, a push approval in the banking app or a hardware key. Set one up at home, because changing methods abroad can be slow.

If a code does not arrive, your home line may have no signal or may be blocked from roaming. Check the line status in your mobile settings, then contact your bank or operator rather than buying extra numbers.

Messaging apps and verification codes

Chat apps run over data, so they work on the travel eSIM without any trouble. Your account stays tied to your home number, and the app simply sends its messages over whichever data line is active.

Sign-in is the exception. When an app asks for a code by SMS or call, it goes to the number on the account, which again means the home SIM. If you are already logged in, you will not notice. If you reinstall an app abroad, you may need the home line to receive a code.

  • Stay logged in on the apps you need before leaving
  • Note which apps send codes by SMS and which use an in-app prompt
  • Keep backup codes where you can reach them without the phone
  • Avoid reinstalling apps in a foreign country if you can

FAQ

Can I keep my phone number with a travel eSIM?

Yes. A data-only travel eSIM has no number of its own and does not replace your SIM. Your home number stays on the home line for calls and text messages.

Will my bank SMS codes still arrive abroad?

Codes go to the number your bank has on file, which is your home number. They arrive if the home SIM can receive texts where you are, so ask your operator and test a code before you travel.

Do I need an unlocked phone for dual SIM?

Apple says that to use two carriers on an iPhone, the carrier must unlock it, unless both plans come from the same carrier. Dual SIM with eSIM needs an iPhone XS, XS Max, XR or later.

Should I leave data roaming on for the home SIM?

Keep it off on the home line, and set the travel eSIM as the line for mobile data. That way the home plan does not bill data you did not mean to use.

Is roaming free in the UK on an EU plan?

Not by default. According to the European Commission, Roam Like At Home does not apply in the UK, and operators may apply fair-use limits elsewhere. Check your own plan with your operator.

Where do I see which line is using data?

On iPhone, open Settings and Mobile Service. On Android, open Settings, Network and Data usage. Look at both lines after the first day of your trip.
